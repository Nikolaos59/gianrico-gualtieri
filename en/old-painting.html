Technique, light, material and memory.

Painting in oils

Light as the origin of the image

The late Middle Ages saw the birth of a new painting technique that gradually replaced egg tempera. Although it enabled a greater degree of naturalism and was used for that purpose, it arose from an essentially symbolic need: how could one show that Light is the origin of visible things? The culture of the period was shaped by reflection on divine Light as a creative force. This found expression in the arts: Gothic architecture gave prominence to windows crossed by light and to stained glass; in painting, this meditation led to the development of oil painting.

The image was made in the following way: the wooden panel used as a support was prepared with a mixture of plaster and glue, then whitened once dry. A drawing was traced on the white chalk ground, sometimes with a silverpoint, sometimes with a brush.

A transparent varnish, ranging in colour from brown to orange and gold, was then spread over the surface. It allowed the brightness of the white to show through while helping the overlying colours cover more effectively.

Colours were applied in glazes, the distinguishing feature of oil painting: transparent layers that never fully covered what lay beneath. In many Flemish paintings, the underlying drawing can still be read through the transparent colour layers.

Each layer had to dry before the next could be applied. A large polyptych therefore often took several years to complete.

Seventeenth-century painting

The height of naturalism

The naturalism of the seventeenth century represents the height of our civilisation. It developed partly from an early recognition of moral and optical truth within Renaissance idealism, and partly in reaction to it. New genres emerged: still lifes of objects, flowers and fruit, banquet tables and, as a particular form of still life, vanitas.

Technically, the Renaissance palette was expanded with Cassel earth and “Mars” colours ranging from yellow through brown, orange and red to a chocolate-like purple, as well as Mars violet, ochres, earth colours and black. Important innovations in yellow came with new pigments from eastern lands.

Chiaroscuro

Light in the composition

The importance of Light, already present at the birth of oil painting, enters the staging of images through chiaroscuro and an overall darker tonality, replacing the very pure, luminous colours of the preceding period.

The artist’s profession reached its highest degree of professionalisation, especially in northern countries, where a talented painter could amass a fortune and live like a respected citizen. Works of art were treated as goods with a market of their own. In countries with a more classical, Mediterranean tradition, art enjoyed an aura of prestige, yet the artist continued to be regarded as a mere craftsman, little different from in the Middle Ages.

The still life

When objects become protagonists

Objects first appeared in painting as symbolic elements, such as lilies and violets representing the virtues of the Virgin. It took a long time for them to move beyond this supporting role and assume an independent one. Skulls appeared on the backs of portraits as a reminder not to boast too much of one’s title or fortune.

The first independent still lifes appeared between the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. The first signed and dated example is from 1602, by Fede Galizia of the Lombard school, although the genre had been documented earlier.

Objects became detached from their context and created their own space, with its own rules, composition and inner logic. No longer mere accessories to a primary meaning, they moved to the foreground as protagonists. Glasses, flowers, skulls, books, bowls and shells began to tell their own stories, fully autonomous yet retaining symbolic meanings that might be more or less easy to decipher.

Vanitas

Vanitas and memento mori

From their earliest appearances on the backs of portraits or in depictions of Calvary and Saint Jerome, skulls gradually became associated with other objects carrying meaning. They formed autonomous images drawing symbolic force from the written and literary sources of the vanitas tradition: Ecclesiastes, the Book of Job, and the many philosophers and writers who addressed the subject from classical antiquity through the Renaissance.

A new subgenre emerged within the young genre of still life: the vanitas. It follows two main paths: vanitas proper, which recalls the fleeting nature of possessions and pleasures; and memento mori, which places greater emphasis on memory and the awareness each person should have of their own mortality.

A few great masters

Sources of inspiration

The seventeenth century is called the Golden Age for good reason. Masters abounded, and their work was often exquisite, thanks in part to rigorous training and in part to a demanding art market. Here are a few flower and vanitas painters who have been a source of inspiration.

01

Nicolas van Verendael (Antwerp, 19 February 1640 – 11 August 1691)

The son of painter Guillaume van Verendael, who taught him the first elements of painting. Apart from a few monkey scenes, he painted chiefly flowers, developing a personal style marked by optical acuity and poetic sensitivity to the delicacy and play of light on petals. “An exceptional painter whom I consider my spiritual master.”

02

Jan Davidsz de Heem (Utrecht, 1606 – Antwerp, 1684)

His early works follow the style of his teacher Balthasar van der Ast (1593/94–1657). De Heem later developed a refined naturalism, both detailed and spirited, in varied compositions: flowers, vanitas, small banquets and formal tables. One of the century’s finest painters, he produced an impressive body of work of consistently high quality.

03

David Bailly (Leiden, 1584–1657)

An active portraitist, he played an essential role in developing vanitas painting, training his nephews and pupils Harmen and Pieter Steenwijck in the genre. Bailly developed an elegant style and created complex compositions, including his self-portrait at two different ages with vanitas objects.

04

Hendrick Andriessen (Antwerp, 1607 – Antwerp or Zeeland, 1655)

He specialised in vanitas, painting some of the finest works of the century. He is thought to have had a congenital malformation; some documents refer to him as “limping Henry”. His work is exceptional both in small compositions and monumental vanitas paintings.
