Pictorial research

Tradition as a living practice

Although Gualtieri’s pictorial research unfolds along several lines of work and explores different possibilities, it can be summed up in one phrase: the hermeneutics of traditional techniques. For him, art is inseparable from technique, beyond any overly idealistic view that devalues manual work.

The reason is simple: art and technique are inseparable, intimately joined, and art can only be reached through the path of technique. To make art, one must learn what art has been—and how it was made.

“Art and technique are inseparable; art can only be reached through the path of technique.”

The seventeenth century

A living memory

Gualtieri draws inspiration from the seventeenth century—the Grand Siècle—with its atmosphere at once austere, carnal and sensual. His research is not confined to it, but revolves around flowers and vanitas, themes dear to the culture of the period.

After a long technical apprenticeship, he first chose historical mimesis: painting vanitas as in the seventeenth century, absorbing the culture of the time, reliving it and presenting it anew as something neither outdated nor over.

Every visual language has a historical component and belongs to a period. Yet for some languages that is not the whole story: they also hold a metaphysical truth beyond time and remain current. Historical mimesis can therefore extend imitation: objects are imitated not only in space but also in time.

To interpret a culture and an era is to choose what matters to us and open an inner dialogue with it. Only on the basis of memory and study can an interpretation build a meaningful bridge between the Grand Siècle, the high point of our civilisation, and the present. It is slow work, carried forward over the years through a practical and inner dialogue with painting.

The concern of art has always been, and will always be, technique. Today more than ever, it must be reclaimed as a fundamental dimension of human life and as the essence of art, beyond any notion of a “message” and any purely intellectual or conceptual interpretation of artistic phenomena.

Technique may be forgotten and serve merely as a means to an end. Or it may turn back on itself and become both path and purpose: the process leading to the final result has value and should be made visible rather than concealed. Memory, awareness, transparency, meditation, and revealing the weave between drawing, colour, plastic presence and trace are guiding principles of the artist’s research.
